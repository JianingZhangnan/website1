Consider an infinitesimal variation:
$$
\delta_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{q}=\epsilon X(\boldsymbol{q},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}},t).
$$
If a classical system don't change under the variation, we call it as a kind of symmetry.

From [[1. Princeple of Least Action#^a4dbb6|Gauge Invariance]] we know, a symmetry can be defined like:
>[!def] infinitesimal symmetry
>An infinitesimal variation of the form
>$$
>\delta_{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{q}=\epsilon X(\boldsymbol{q},\dot{\boldsymbol{q}},t).
>$$
>is called an **infinitesimal symmetry** of $S$ if the variation of the Lagrangian is a total time derivative
> $$
> \delta_{\epsilon}\mathcal{L} = \epsilon\left( X\dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial q } +\dot{X}\dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial \dot{q} }  \right)=\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \mathscr{l}
> $$

>[!example] a time invariance
>Let $\mathcal{L}=\dfrac{1}{2}m\dot{q}^{2}-V(q)$. Consider the infinitesimal time transformation $t\to t+\epsilon$.
Then
> $$
> \delta_{\epsilon}q = \epsilon \dot{q},\quad \delta_{\epsilon}\dot{q} = \epsilon \ddot{q}
> $$
> $$
> \delta_{\epsilon}\mathcal{L} = \epsilon\left( \dot{q}\dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial q } +\ddot{q}\dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial \dot{q} }  \right)=\epsilon \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \left( \dfrac{1}{2}m\dot{q}^{2}-V \right)
> $$
> On the other hand,
> $$
> S = \int \left( \dfrac{1}{2}m\dot{q}^{2}-V(q) \right)\mathrm{d}t
> $$
> is obviously invariant under the transformation
> $$
> S[q(t)]=S[q(t+t')]
> $$
> 

# Nother's Theorem

The corresponding $\text{symmetry}\iff \text{converse}$ is too well known to consume more words. But in this section we'd like to prove it more trickly.

Assume the infinitesimal variation $\delta_{\epsilon}q=\epsilon X$ is an **infinitesimal symmetry**. Let's consider a more general form:
$$
\delta_{\epsilon(t)}q=\epsilon(t)X
$$
by promoting $\epsilon=\epsilon(t)$ to be time dependent. Then
$$
\begin{align}
\delta_{\epsilon(t)}\mathcal{L}  & = \dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial q } \delta_{\epsilon(t)}q+\dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial \dot{q} } \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} (\delta_{\epsilon(t)}q)  \\
 & = \epsilon(t)\left( X\dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial q } +\dot{X} \dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial \dot{q} }  \right)+\dot{\epsilon}(t)X\dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial \dot{q} } .
\end{align}
$$

From above we know $X\dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial q } +\dot{X} \dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial \dot{q} }=\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathscr{l}$. So
$$
\delta_{\epsilon(t)}\mathcal{L} = \epsilon(t)\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \mathscr{l}+\dot{\epsilon}(t)X\dfrac{ \partial \mathcal{L} }{ \partial \dot{q} } 
$$

Integrating over time and assuming $\epsilon(t_i)=\epsilon(t_f)=0$, we have on-shell ($\delta_{\epsilon(t)}S=0$):
$$
\begin{align}
\delta_{\epsilon(t)} S &= \int_{t_i}^{t_f} \left( \epsilon(t)\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{l}}{\mathrm{d}t} + \dot{\epsilon}(t) X \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}} \right) \mathrm{d}t \\
&= \int_{t_i}^{t_f} \epsilon(t)\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{l}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\mathrm{d}t + \left.\epsilon(t)X\dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}}\right|_{t_i}^{t_f} - \int_{t_i}^{t_f} \epsilon(t)\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(X\dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}}\right)\mathrm{d}t \\
&= \int_{t_i}^{t_f} \epsilon(t)\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\mathscr{l} - X\dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}}\right)\mathrm{d}t = 0.
\end{align}
$$

Since $\epsilon(t)$ is arbitrary, $J$ is conserved:
$$
J = \mathscr{l} - X\dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}}, \qquad \dfrac{\mathrm{d}J}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0.
$$
