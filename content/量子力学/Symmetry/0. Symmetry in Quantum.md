First let's think about an **operation** on the whole system. For example, spatial translation operator, time-reversal operator, rotation ... . Now we treat them as **active** transfromation for all the state vectors:
$$
\ket{\psi'} = U\ket{\psi }
$$
So, what is symmetry? First, let's think it form, the Hamiltonian. We know the Hamiltonian is the key of a quantum system, if the Hamiltonian keeps when we apply $U$ to the system, then we regard the system as "not changed":
$$
[H,U]=0\iff HU=UH \iff U \text{ is a symmetry of the system}
$$
But later, we would see that there is an implicit assumption underlying the statement above. That is, $U$ is linear and hermitian. Whereas there is other operators like, time reverse operator, is anti-linear and ant-hermitian. So let's go back to the basic conception:

# Wigner's Theorem

> [!def] Symmetry Transformation on Rays
> A physical state corresponds to a **ray** $\mathbf{R} = \{ e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle : \alpha \in \mathbb{R} \}$ in Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$. A **symmetry transformation** $T$ maps rays to rays ($\mathbf{R} \mapsto \mathbf{R}' = T\mathbf{R}$) such that all transition probabilities are preserved:
>
> $$
> |\langle \phi' | \psi' \rangle|^2 = |\langle \phi | \psi \rangle|^2
> $$
>
> for any representatives $|\psi\rangle \in \mathbf{R}_1,\, |\phi\rangle \in \mathbf{R}_2$ and $|\psi'\rangle \in T\mathbf{R}_1,\, |\phi'\rangle \in T\mathbf{R}_2$.

> [!done] Wigner's Theorem
> Any symmetry transformation $T$ on $\mathcal{H}$ can be realized by an operator $U : \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}$ (i.e., $U|\psi\rangle \in T\mathbf{R}$ for $|\psi\rangle \in \mathbf{R}$) that is either:
> 1. **Unitary (linear)**: $U(a|\psi\rangle + b|\phi\rangle) = aU|\psi\rangle + bU|\phi\rangle$ and $\langle U\phi | U\psi \rangle = \langle \phi | \psi \rangle$, or
> 2. **Antiunitary (antilinear)**: $U(a|\psi\rangle + b|\phi\rangle) = a^* U|\psi\rangle + b^* U|\phi\rangle$ and $\langle U\phi | U\psi \rangle = \langle \phi | \psi \rangle^*$.
>
> Moreover, $U$ is unique up to an overall phase factor.

## Proof

1. **Orthonormal basis and relative phases.** Pick an orthonormal basis $\{|e_n\rangle\}$ of $\mathcal{H}$ and representatives $\{|e_n'\rangle\}$ of $T\mathbf{R}_{e_n}$; probability preservation gives $|\langle e_m'|e_n'\rangle|^2 = \delta_{mn}$, so $\{|e_n'\rangle\}$ is orthonormal. To fix the arbitrary relative phase between $|e_n'\rangle$ ($n \ge 2$) and $|e_1'\rangle$, consider $|u_n\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|e_1\rangle + |e_n\rangle)$: matching overlaps with $|e_k\rangle$ forces $|u_n'\rangle = c_1|e_1'\rangle + c_n|e_n'\rangle$ with $|c_1| = |c_n| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, so absorbing the phases into $|u_n'\rangle$ and $|e_n'\rangle$ yields
   $$
   |u_n'\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|e_1'\rangle + |e_n'\rangle\big) \qquad (n \ge 2).
   $$

2. **Coefficients of an arbitrary state.** For $|\psi\rangle = \sum_n a_n|e_n\rangle$ (taking $a_1 > 0$ by adjusting the ray representative) and $|\psi'\rangle = \sum_n a_n'|e_n'\rangle$, overlaps with $|e_n\rangle$ give $|a_n'| = |a_n|$ (fix $a_1' = a_1$), and $|\langle u_n'|\psi'\rangle|^2 = |\langle u_n|\psi\rangle|^2$ gives $\operatorname{Re}(a_n') = \operatorname{Re}(a_n)$. Hence $a_n' = a_n$ or $a_n^*$ for each $n$.

3. **Global consistency.** Applying Step 2 to $|\alpha_n\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(|e_1\rangle + i|e_2\rangle + |e_n\rangle)$ and $|\beta_n\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(|e_1\rangle + |e_2\rangle + i|e_n\rangle)$ ($n \ge 3$) gives $|\alpha_n'\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(|e_1'\rangle + \eta_2 i|e_2'\rangle + |e_n'\rangle)$ and $|\beta_n'\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(|e_1'\rangle + |e_2'\rangle + \eta_n i|e_n'\rangle)$ with $\eta_2, \eta_n \in \{\pm 1\}$. Matching $|\langle \beta_n'|\alpha_n'\rangle|^2 = \frac{1}{9}$ forces $\eta_n = \eta_2 =: \eta$. Checking $|\langle \beta_n'|\psi'\rangle|^2$ then forces $\operatorname{Im}(a_n') = \eta\operatorname{Im}(a_n)$ for all $|\psi\rangle$, so either $a_n' = a_n$ ($\eta = +1$) or $a_n' = a_n^*$ ($\eta = -1$) for every $n$ and every state (extending to $a_1 = 0$ by using another nonzero component).

4. **Operator construction and uniqueness.** Defining $U|\psi\rangle = \sum_n a_n|e_n'\rangle$ ($\eta=+1$) or $\sum_n a_n^*|e_n'\rangle$ ($\eta=-1$), orthonormality of $\{|e_n'\rangle\}$ directly gives $\langle U\phi|U\psi\rangle = \langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ (unitary) or $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle^*$ (antiunitary). If $\tilde{U}$ also implements $T$, then $\tilde{U}|e_n\rangle = e^{i\theta_n}U|e_n\rangle$, and applying $\tilde{U}$ to $|e_1\rangle + |e_n\rangle$ forces $e^{i\theta_n} = e^{i\theta_1}$ for all $n$, so $\tilde{U} = e^{i\theta_1} U$. $\blacksquare$
