## Basis of $S_{b/f}\mathcal H^N$

We have the [[1. Basic Space#^def-basic-space|product basis]] $\{|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N)\}$ of $\mathcal H^N$. How to find the basis of a subspace? Let us break it down from two different perspectives.

### 1. Standard linear algebra

In fact, the notation $S_{b/f}\mathcal H^N$ means $\operatorname{range}S_{b/f}$; see [[2. Symmetrizer and Antisymmetrizer#^prop-physical-space-image|the projection images]].

In linear algebra, if $\{v_i\}$ is a basis, then the image of a linear map $T$ is spanned by $\{Tv_i\}$: applying $T$ to $v=\sum_i c_i v_i$ gives $Tv=\sum_i c_iTv_i$. Thus

$$
\operatorname{range}S_{b/f}
=\operatorname{span}\{S_{b/f}|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N)\}
=\operatorname{span}\left\{\dfrac1{N!}\sum_P(\pm)^P P|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N)\right\}.
$$

Here $\operatorname{span}$ means all linear combinations (and their norm limits in the Hilbert space). But how can we pick out a maximal independent set? It seems difficult.

### 2. Occupation Number

Forget the image space for a moment and consider physics in $S_{b/f}\mathcal H^N$. Since particles are identical, product states differing only by a permutation give the same physical state after projection. For Bose the projected vectors are equal; for Fermi they differ by the sign of the permutation.

For example, $\psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_2)$ and $\psi_1(x_2)\psi_2(x_1)$ would be projected to the same Bose state, or to opposite Fermi vectors representing the same physical state. Here $\psi_i(x)$ is the wavefunction of the single-particle state $i$.

Naturally, we could introduce:

> [!def] Occupation number
> $n_\lambda$ is the number of occurrences of the single-particle label $\lambda$ in $|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N)$. Thus $\sum_\lambda n_\lambda=N$.
^def-occupation-number

For example, in $|\alpha,\alpha,\beta,\alpha)$, $n_\alpha=3$, $n_\beta=1$, $n_\gamma=0$. And

$$
S_b|\alpha,\alpha,\beta,\alpha)=S_b|\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\alpha)=\cdots.
$$

So in $\mathcal B^N$ and $\mathcal F^N$, $\{n_\lambda\}$ can label the different basis states. It does not specify a general linear combination of them.

> [!done] Pauli exclusion principle
> In $\mathcal F^N$, $n_\lambda=0$ or $1$. A product state with a repeated single-particle label has zero antisymmetrization.
^prop-pauli-exclusion

If $\lambda_i=\lambda_j=\lambda$, then $P_{ij}|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N)=|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N)$. In the antisymmetrizer sum, pair each $P$ with $PP_{ij}$. They act identically on this product state but have opposite signs, so the two contributions sum to zero:

$$
S_f|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda\cdots\lambda\cdots\lambda_N)=0.
$$

## Constructing the basis

From the two perspectives above, we can construct the basis of $\mathcal B^N$ and $\mathcal F^N$.

### 1. Bose case

> [!def] Normalized Bose basis
> For an occupation list $\{n_\lambda\}$ with $\sum_\lambda n_\lambda=N$, choose any product state having that list and let
> $$
> |n_1n_2\cdots\rangle
> :=\left(\dfrac{N!}{\prod_\lambda n_\lambda!}\right)^{1/2}
> S_b|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N).
> $$
> The same normalized vector is also written $|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N\rangle$; its order does not matter in the Bose case.
^def-bose-basis

You can also ignore the normalization factor at first and add it later. To find it, use $S_b^\dagger S_b=S_b$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\lambda'_1\cdots\lambda'_N|S_b^\dagger S_b|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N)
&=\dfrac1{N!}\sum_P(\lambda'_1\cdots\lambda'_N|\lambda_{P1}\cdots\lambda_{PN})\\
&=\dfrac1{N!}\sum_P\prod_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda'_i,\lambda_{Pi}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The product is $1$ exactly when the permutation matches the two ordered lists. If the occupation numbers differ, no permutation does so. If they agree, there are $\prod_\lambda n_\lambda!$ matching permutations, because the $n_\lambda$ equal labels may be permuted among themselves. Therefore:

> [!done] Orthonormality and completeness of the Bose basis
> $$
> \langle n'_1n'_2\cdots|n_1n_2\cdots\rangle
> =\prod_\lambda\delta_{n'_\lambda,n_\lambda},
> $$
> $$
> \sum_{\{n_\lambda\}:\,\sum_\lambda n_\lambda=N}
> |\{n_\lambda\}\rangle\langle\{n_\lambda\}|=I_{\mathcal B^N}.
> $$
> Each occupation list is counted once.
^prop-bose-basis

Completeness follows because every projected product state is proportional to one of these vectors, and the projected product states span $\mathcal B^N$.

### 2. Fermi case

Fix an order of the single-particle labels. To select independent basis states, use only lists $\lambda_1<\cdots<\lambda_N$.

> [!def] Normalized Fermi basis
> For distinct single-particle labels, let
> $$
> |\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N\rangle:=\sqrt{N!}\,S_f|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N).
> $$
> Exchanging two labels changes the sign. A list with repeated labels denotes the zero vector. The independent basis uses the fixed ordering $\lambda_1<\cdots<\lambda_N$.
^def-fermi-basis

By $S_f^\dagger S_f=S_f$,

$$
\langle\lambda'_1\cdots\lambda'_N|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N\rangle
=\sum_P(-1)^P\prod_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda'_i,\lambda_{Pi}}.
$$

For two distinct-label lists, at most one permutation matches them.

> [!done] Orthonormality and completeness of the Fermi basis
> For arbitrary distinct-label lists, write
> $$
> \langle\lambda'_1\cdots\lambda'_N|\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N\rangle
> =\widetilde\delta_{\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N}^{\lambda'_1\cdots\lambda'_N}
> =\begin{cases}
> +1,&\text{the primed list is an even permutation of the unprimed list},\\
> -1,&\text{the primed list is an odd permutation of the unprimed list},\\
> 0,&\text{otherwise}.
> \end{cases}
> $$
> Restricting both lists to the fixed ordering makes the basis orthonormal, and
> $$
> \sum_{\lambda_1<\cdots<\lambda_N}
> |\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N\rangle\langle\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_N|
> =I_{\mathcal F^N}.
> $$
^prop-fermi-basis

Every nonzero antisymmetrized product state differs from one of the ordered states only by a sign. They therefore span $\mathcal F^N$.

### Example: Four states, three particles

Let the single-particle states be $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\mu$, in this order.

For Bose, the basis in occupation-number notation is

$$
\begin{gathered}
|3000\rangle,\ |2100\rangle,\ |2010\rangle,\ |2001\rangle,\ |1200\rangle,\\
|0210\rangle,\ |0201\rangle,\ |1020\rangle,\ |0120\rangle,\ |0021\rangle,\\
|1002\rangle,\ |0102\rangle,\ |0012\rangle,\ |0300\rangle,\ |0030\rangle,\\
|0003\rangle,\ |1110\rangle,\ |1101\rangle,\ |1011\rangle,\ |0111\rangle.
\end{gathered}
$$

There are $\binom{3+4-1}{4-1}=20$ states.

For Fermi, the basis is

$$
|\alpha\beta\gamma\rangle,\quad|\alpha\beta\mu\rangle,\quad
|\alpha\gamma\mu\rangle,\quad|\beta\gamma\mu\rangle.
$$

There are $\binom43=4$ states.

> [!done] Number of basis states
> For $m$ single-particle states and $N$ particles,
> $$
> \dim\mathcal B^N=\binom{m+N-1}{m-1},\qquad
> \dim\mathcal F^N=\binom mN.
> $$
> The Fermi space is zero when $N>m$.
^prop-basis-count

For Bose, arrange $N$ particles and $m-1$ separators in a row; the numbers between adjacent separators give the $m$ occupation numbers. For Fermi, simply choose $N$ distinct states from $m$.
